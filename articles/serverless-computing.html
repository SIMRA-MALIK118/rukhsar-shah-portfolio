Serverless Computing: Why Startups Keep Choosing It (and When It Backfires)

Sample article · 4 min read

"Serverless" is a bad name for what it actually does — there are still servers, you just stop thinking about them. You write a function, upload it, and the cloud provider handles the rest: provisioning, scaling, patching, taking it down to zero when nobody's using it. For a small team, that trade is often an easy yes.

What you're actually buying

Not "no infrastructure." You're buying the removal of infrastructure decisions from your daily workload. No one is picking instance sizes, setting up auto-scaling rules, or getting paged because a server ran out of memory at 2 a.m. The platform absorbs that layer, and your team spends its time on the code that actually differentiates the product.

That's the real appeal for an early-stage startup: engineering time is the scarcest resource in the building, and serverless spends less of it on plumbing.

Where it genuinely wins

  • Spiky, unpredictable traffic. A function that runs 40 times a day and 40,000 times during a launch scales itself — no one has to pre-provision for the spike.
  • Small, independent tasks. Image processing, webhook handlers, scheduled jobs — work that doesn't need a persistent process running in the background.
  • Early-stage cost. You pay per invocation, not for an idle server sitting there waiting for traffic that hasn't arrived yet.

Where it quietly stops making sense

The pattern that catches teams off guard is scale. Serverless pricing is efficient at low and irregular volume and gets expensive once traffic is high and constant — at that point, you're paying a per-request premium for elasticity you no longer need, because the traffic isn't spiky anymore, it's just large.

Serverless is a great answer to "we don't know how much traffic we'll get." It's a worse answer to "we already know, and it's a lot."

Cold starts are the other recurring complaint — a function that hasn't run recently can take a noticeable moment to spin up, which matters for anything latency-sensitive, like a user-facing API on the critical path of checkout.

The practical version

Most teams that get value from serverless don't run their whole product on it. They use it for the bursty, event-driven pieces — and fall back to conventional infrastructure for the steady, high-volume core. It's a tool for a specific shape of workload, not a replacement for thinking about architecture at all.
