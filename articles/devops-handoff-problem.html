DevOps Isn't a Job Title — It's a Handoff Problem Most Teams Still Haven't Fixed

Sample article · 4 min read

Most companies that say they "do DevOps" mean they hired someone with DevOps in their title and gave them the deploy pipeline to own. That's not what the term was supposed to describe. DevOps started as a response to a specific, painful handoff: developers threw finished code over a wall, and a separate operations team caught it, deployed it, and got paged when it broke.

The wall, not the tools

The tools that get associated with DevOps — CI/CD pipelines, infrastructure as code, containers — are real and useful. But they're solutions to a coordination problem, not the problem itself. You can adopt every one of them and still have the same wall: a team that writes code and a team that keeps it running, with a ticket queue between them and no shared accountability for what happens after deploy.

Hiring one "DevOps engineer" to sit between those two teams often just adds a person to hand off to — a narrower wall instead of no wall.

What actually closes the gap

  • Developers carry a pager. The team that writes the code is also the team that gets woken up when it breaks. That single change reshapes what gets shipped, because the cost of a bad deploy is felt immediately by the person who caused it.
  • Deploys are small and frequent. A change that ships alone is easy to trace if something breaks. A change bundled with forty others is a debugging nightmare no tooling fixes.
  • Observability is a day-one requirement, not a nice-to-have. If you can't see what a system is doing in production, you're debugging blind regardless of how good your pipeline is.

Why this is a harder sell than a tool

Buying a platform is a purchase order. Changing who's accountable when something breaks is a culture change — and culture changes don't fit in a sprint.

That's the real reason so many "DevOps transformations" stall at the tooling stage. Standing up a Kubernetes cluster is a project with a start and end date. Making engineers genuinely own production outcomes is an ongoing negotiation about responsibility, and it's uncomfortable in a way that adopting new software isn't.

Where to actually start

Not with a platform migration. With one team, one service, and one clear rule: whoever writes it deploys it and gets paged for it. Everything else — the pipelines, the dashboards, the infrastructure automation — exists to make that rule survivable, not to replace it.
